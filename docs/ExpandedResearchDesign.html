Expanded Research Design

Introduction

How does colonial extraction affect contemporary healthcare access? A dominant theme in the historical development literature posits that historical extraction, characterized by the slave trade and colonial rule, is a fundamental cause of modern underdevelopment(Nunn 2008). Scholarship by Nathan Nunn has demonstrated a robust negative relationship between the number of slaves exported from a territory and its current economic relationships between the number of slaves exported from a territory and its current economic performance, arguing that the poorest African countries today are those that were most heavily targeted for extraction(Nunn 2008). Similarly, Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson contend that European powers had established “extractive states” in regions where high settler mortality made a permanent residence unfeasible(Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001). These states were designed to transfer resources to the metropole rather than protect private property or provide public goods, including healthcare, leaving a legacy of weak institutions that continue to stifle growth[Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001)](Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2020).

These country-level analyses, however, often assume a uniform national effect and overlook the substantial within-country variation that characterizes African states(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). While national institutions may correlate with wealth, particularly near the capital city, their influence often decays significantly as one moves into the hinterland(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). Michalopoulos and Papaioannou have advanced this subnational perspective, arguing that Africa frequently operates under”dual institutional systems” where formal national laws are secondary to the effect of local ethnic norms in remote regions(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). Their research has revealed that for many partitioned ethnicities, differences in countrywide institutional structures across national borders do not explain differences in local development. This is suggestive of a critical gap in current understanding: if national institutions failed to penetrate the interior, what historical forces determine a household’s ability to access vital services such as healthcare(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014).

This paper addresses this gap by shifting the unit of analysis to ethnic homelands and household clusters within Nigeria. While prior studies primarily utilize aggregate measures like real per capita GDP or satellite-recorded light density to proxy for development, this study examines individual well-being through the lens of self-reported healthcare barriers(Nunn 2008). This focus allows for a distinction between institutional capacity, the quality of the healthcare system itself, and household-level access, the ability of individuals to traverse financial and logistical constraints(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). 

Building on earlier contributions, this analysis proposes a mechanism of spatially uneven economic and administrative persistence post-colonial presence. Because colonial extraction was often geographically concentrated around specific trade routes, administrative centers, and export infrastructure, it may have generated localized economic advantages that outlived the colonial era itself(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). By leveraging subnational variation, this paper investigates whether households in historically high-extraction regions possess higher levels of wealth and better proximity to infrastructure, thereby facing fewer barriers to accessing care(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2020). In doing so, it extends the subnational development literature to individual healthcare outcomes, offering a more nuanced understanding of how the shadow of history continues to shape the livelihoods of post-colonial communities today(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2016).

Hypothesis

While prior literature has emphasized that extractive institutions reduce long-run economic development and institutional quality at the country-level, this analysis focuses on within-country variation and household-level outcomes[Nunn (2008)](Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001). In doing so, it builds on recent work by Michalopoulos and Papaioannou, who demonstrate that national institutions often have limited explanatory power for economic outcomes within partitioned ethnic homelands(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). Their findings suggest that development patterns in Africa are shaped not only by national-scale institutional quality, but also by localized historical and economic conditions(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2016, 2014).

This insight is central to the framework of the following discussion. Nigeria, like many of its African peer countries, exhibits substantial spatial heterogeneity in economic activity and state presence(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). Similarly, colonial extraction during the was not uniformly distributed across the country(Nunn 2008). In fact, extraction was concentrated in regions with access to trade routes, administrative centers, and export infrastructure (Nunn 2008). Consistent with prior work on the persistence of colonial presence, during the colonial period, due to the Western focus on areas of high extraction, these regions became more economically integrated(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2020).

Therefore, the primary hypothesis of this work is that higher levels of historical colonial extraction (measured as log slave exports at the ethnic homeland level) are associated with a lower probability that individuals report barriers to accessing healthcare.

The mechanism underlying this hypothesis operates through localized economic persistence rather than national institutional quality, as previously possessed(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2020). In line with prior research focused on local dynamics dominating national institutions, this paper posits that extraction-driven economic differences persist independently of national policy environments(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2014). Regions historically exposed to extraction developed greater integration into trade and administrative networks, resulting in higher contemporary levels of household wealth(Michalopoulos and Papaioannou 2020). Leading to the second hypothesis of this piece: higher levels of historical extraction are associated with higher contemporary household wealth at the cluster level. 

Household wealth, in turn, plays a central role in determining access to healthcare. The dependent variables used in this analysis, such as inability to afford treatment, lack of drugs, absence of providers, and difficulties gaining permission, capture self-reported barriers rather than objective measures of healthcare capacity. As such, along with a reflection of how individuals traverse healthcare systems, they reflect an individual’s ability to navigate financial, geographic, and social constraints(Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001). Wealthier households are better able to overcome these constraints, even in settings where healthcare systems are weaker or unevenly distributed, implying that higher household wealth is associated with a lower probability of reporting barriers to healthcare access.

Taken together, these hypotheses imply the following mechanism at work: 

Extraction → Localized Economic Advantage → Higher Household Wealth → Lower Reported Barriers

Data & Discussion

This analysis focuses on Nigeria (2008) and combines historical and contemporary datasets to examine whether colonial extraction has an effect on present-day healthcare access. The primary independent variable is historical colonial extraction, measured as the natural logarithm of total exports associated with each ethnic homeland (ln_total_exports), following the approach of prior literature. This variable captures the intensity of historical exposure to extractive economic systems.

The main dependent variables are a set of binary indicators of self-reported barriers to healthcare access, derived from DHS survey responses. These variables take the value of 1 if the respondent reports a given factor as a “big problem” in accessing healthcare, and a 0 otherwise. The specific outcomes include:

  • bhcmoney (cost barrier)

  • bhcdrug (lack of medicines)

  • bhcnoprov (lack of providers)

  • bhcnofemdr (lack of female doctor)

  • bhcpermit (need permission)

  • bhcdistance (distance barrier)

  • bhctaketran (transportation barrier)

These measures capture perceived constraints to accessing care, rather than objective healthcare system capacity. 

To account for key confounders, the analysis includes controls for mean household wealth (mean_wealth) at the cluster level, constructed from DHS wealth index (continuous measure, wealths) and urbanization (mean_urban), capturing the share of urban households within each cluster.

Research Design

To test the relationship between historical colonial extraction and contemporary barriers to healthcare access, this paper estimates the following baseline regression at the DHS cluster level:

\[ \text{Barrier}_c = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(\text{Extraction}_e) + \beta_2 \text{Wealth}_c + \beta_3 \text{Urban}_c + \epsilon_c \]

Where Barrier_c represents the average probability that individuals in cluster c report a given healthcare access constraint, ln(Extraction_e) is the log of historical slave exports associated with the ethnic homeland e in which cluster c is located, Wealth_c is the mean cluster-level wealth, and Urban_c captures the share of urban households in the cluster.

The coefficient of interest, β_1 , captures the relationship between historical extraction and contemporary healthcare access, after conditioning on key observable characteristics. Wealth is included as a control because it is a primary determinant of healthcare access and represents a key mediating channel through which extraction may operate. Controlling for wealth enables the analysis to distinguish between the indirect effect of extraction through household economic resources and any remaining direct effect operating through other mechanisms, such as infrastructure or service availability. Urbanization is included as it accounts for differences in access driven by population density, proximity to healthcare facilities, and the concentration of public services available in urban areas. 

While the regression framework indeed provides evidence of robust association, the interpretation of β_1 as a causal effect is subject to several important limitations. First, historical extraction may be correlated with geographic characteristics, such as proximity to the coast and trade routes, which themselves can influence contemporary access to healthcare. Secondly, regions exposed to higher extraction may have experienced differential investment in infrastructure or administrative capacity during the colonial period, which could persist independently of extraction intensity. Thirdly, there may be selection effects, whereby populations with different socioeconomic characteristics sort into or out of historically advantaged regions through time. Finally, unobserved factors such as cultural norms and historical conflict could influence both extraction intensity and present-day healthcare access, leading to an omitted variable bias. 

To strengthen the credibility of the proposed mechanism, this paper implements a placebo test using mean cluster-level education as the outcome variable. If the primary mechanism operates through localized economic and access-related channels, such as wealth and infrastructure, then extraction should not have a direct effect on outcomes that are less immediately tied to access constraints, once wealth is controlled for.


The placebo regression model is taken as:

\[ \text{Education}_c = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(\text{Extraction}_e) + \beta_2 \text{Wealth}_c + \beta_3 \text{Urban}_c + \epsilon_c \]

The results (as seen in Table 4) show that the coefficient of extraction is negative but statistically insignificant, while wealth remains strongly positive and highly significant. This pattern suggests that extraction does not independently predict education outcomes once wealth is accounted for.

Figure 3 plots mean years of education against historical extraction and shows no statistically significant relationship, supporting the placebo test that extraction does not generally predict human capital outcomes once wealth is controlled for.

This placebo test is helpful as it rules out the alternative explanation that extraction is simply capturing a broad, generalizable form of development advantage across all outcomes. If extraction were acting as a proxy for overall socioeconomic advancement, we would expect it to significantly predict education as well. Instead, the absence of a significant relationship is supportive of the interpretation that extraction affects healthcare access through specific channels related to economic resources and access environments, rather than through universal improvements in human capital or development.

All in all, the research design provides evidence consistent with a mechanism in which colonial extraction shapes contemporary healthcare access through both wealth-based pathways and persistent local structural advantages, while the placebo test strengthens confidence that the observed relationships are not driven by a general development gradient.
